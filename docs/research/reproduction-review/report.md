The three investigations ask different bounded questions. Quality studies same-input prefix reuse under quality, parity, cost and relative-tail gates. Candidate studies uncached symbolic omission and a learned refusal rule: preserved program semantics do not preserve model answers. Systems studies omission against an already-cached full-context comparator, including first-use versus subsequent-query input work and matched model/context transfer. Shared grammar, software and hardware connect these findings; they are not three novel mechanisms or general task-solving advances.

The candidate's prefix control loses the symbolic answer on every one of its 32 fit tables. This particular nonsemantic control cannot establish superiority over all shortening policies. The manuscript correctly makes that limit explicit. D-prefixed development and C-prefixed confirmation also use separately derived random streams: new groups do not establish IID exchangeability or lexical invariance. Repeated outputs are not new independent tasks, and observed either-answer-correct ceilings consume both answers and the known target; they are not deployable selectors or limits on new outputs.

The restricted grammar is directly solvable by a symbolic interpreter. All three packages state that limitation. Quality's batch-throughput result cannot become an interactive-serving claim when neither arm meets its joint SLO. Candidate and systems fail absolute quality and cannot support eligible efficiency claims. Input-token counts are not FLOPs, energy, monetary cost or whole-host measurements. Attempt wall, archive-process execution, queue waiting, inherited setup and original-allocation elapsed time are overlapping scopes that must not be added.
